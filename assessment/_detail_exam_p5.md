## P5a-1

Begin at the receptor, before introducing Ras. Ligand binding promotes dimerization or a rearrangement that brings the receptor's cytosolic kinase domains into an activating relationship. These domains belong to the RTK itself; the pathway does not need Ras to act as the initiating kinase.

Each receptor unit phosphorylates tyrosines on its partner. The across-partner action is what trans identifies in trans-autophosphorylation. The resulting phosphorylated receptor can recruit intracellular signaling proteins and propagate the response. Explain the ligand-dependent structural change, the intrinsic kinase action, and the partner relationship rather than simply saying the receptor turns on. Retaining dimerization/rearrangement also avoids claiming that every RTK must begin as an isolated monomer in exactly the same structural arrangement.

## P5a-2

The quoted account puts a downstream switch at the beginning and gives it the wrong chemistry. Ras is a GTPase, not the protein kinase that initiates RTK phosphorylation. The receptor starts the sequence when ligand binding promotes an activating dimerization or rearrangement. Its own intracellular tyrosine-kinase domains then act across the receptor pair.

Each unit phosphorylates tyrosines on the other, producing the activated phosphorylated complex. Recruitment of the adaptor and exchange-factor machinery follows, leading later to Ras activation. Reconstructing that order makes clear why Ras cannot be substituted for the receptor's intrinsic enzymatic activity. It also separates two kinds of switching: receptor phosphorylation and Ras nucleotide exchange. Both are important, but their positions and molecular operations differ.

## P5a-3

Being brought together positions the receptors' intracellular kinase domains to act on one another. One domain phosphorylates tyrosine residues on the partner receptor, and the partner can reciprocate. Trans therefore means across the pair rather than only modification of the same receptor unit's own residues.

The consequence is not simply physical contact with no biochemical change. The phosphorylation contributes to an activated receptor complex with sites that can recruit the downstream relay. Keep ligand recognition outside the cell separate from kinase action on the cytosolic side; the extracellular ligand is not itself performing the phosphorylation. The question's paired-monomer description supplies a concrete example of the dimerization/rearrangement principle used by the course. No Ras kinase step is needed to initiate this receptor event.

## P5b-1

Expand the acronym and connect it to a molecular operation: **guanine nucleotide exchange factor**. Exchange allows GDP to leave Ras and GTP to bind in its place. It is not addition of phosphate to GDP while GDP remains attached to the switch.

The factor named in this pathway is **Sos, Son of Sevenless**, linked to the activated receptor through the adaptor arrangement. Sos favors the GTP-bound ON state, but it is distinct from Ras itself and from the downstream kinases. Contrast its role with GAP: GAP accelerates GTP hydrolysis and the return toward OFF. Naming Sos alone answers only the identity part; explaining nucleotide release and replacement shows why it is classified as a GEF and how it helps activate Ras.

## P5b-2

The report confuses exchange with phosphorylation. Sos is a **GEF for Ras**, not a kinase that manufactures GTP from the GDP already bound to Ras. Its action promotes GDP release, after which GTP can bind. Changing the bound nucleotide favors the active Ras conformation.

This distinction matters because it assigns the correct job to each pathway component. Grb2 provides an adaptor link, Sos provides exchange-factor activity, Ras supplies a nucleotide-sensitive switch, and downstream Raf/MEK/ERK supply kinase activities. The presence of many phosphorylation steps later in the pathway does not make every earlier arrow a phosphate-transfer reaction. A precise correction gives Sos's expanded class name and describes release followed by replacement, rather than merely changing the label kinase to an unexplained abbreviation.

## P5b-3

The starting state in the question is Ras–GDP, the OFF state in the course model. Recruitment of Sos brings the **guanine nucleotide exchange factor** into position to promote GDP release. GTP can then occupy the switch, favoring Ras activation and subsequent effector interaction.

No direct conversion of bound GDP into GTP is required. That would be a different chemical operation from the exchange described. Also avoid assigning the reverse action to Sos: acceleration of GTP hydrolysis is the GAP-associated OFF transition, not the GEF step. Reading the initial nucleotide state and the named accessory factor together tells you which direction of the cycle is being favored. This explains how recruitment at a receptor complex can lead to activation of a separate intracellular switch.

## P5c-1

Write the cycle with the bound nucleotide shown at every stage: **Ras–GDP → exchange → Ras–GTP → effector interaction → GTP hydrolysis → Ras–GDP**. GEF facilitates the exchange stage by promoting GDP release. The resulting active Ras can bind an effector such as Raf. Ras's hydrolytic activity then converts its bound GTP to GDP, with GAP accelerating that step.

The two accessory factors therefore regulate different transitions. GEF favors entry into the active state, while GAP speeds the chemical reaction associated with returning toward OFF. The word activating in GAP refers to GTPase activity, not sustained downstream signaling. Hydrolysis is also not identical to nucleotide dissociation. After hydrolysis, a later exchange event is needed for reactivation, which is why both halves of the cycle must be described.

## P5c-2

Impaired hydrolysis affects the return route, not the initial exchange requirement. Once Ras has acquired GTP, a slower GTP-to-GDP conversion lets its active state and effector interaction persist longer. This explains sustained downstream signaling without claiming that Ras has become a kinase or that GDP-bound Ras is automatically active.

The normal cycle starts with GEF-promoted GDP release and GTP binding. Ras–GTP interacts with an effector, then intrinsic GTP hydrolysis—accelerated by GAP—returns it toward Ras–GDP. Subsequent exchange can activate it again. The mutant disrupts that normal timing of shutdown. The word impaired supports prolonged activity rather than a universal claim of permanent ON under every condition. Explaining the normal transitions alongside the defect identifies precisely why this mutation differs from loss of the GEF.

## P5c-3

Increased GAP activity accelerates the hydrolysis step: **Ras-bound GTP → GDP**. Because the GTP-bound conformation is ON, faster conversion favors a quicker return toward the GDP-bound OFF state and a shorter active period. GAP does not perform GDP release followed by GTP binding; that is the GEF-controlled exchange step.

Place this change within the whole cycle. GEF first helps activate Ras by exchange, Ras–GTP can then engage an effector, and GAP speeds the later hydrolytic return. Afterward, exchange is needed again for reactivation. Thus increasing a GTPase-activating protein can reduce signaling duration rather than increase it: the activated enzyme activity is hydrolysis. No numerical duration can be calculated without rate information, but the direction of the timing change follows from the affected reaction.

## P5d-1

Reconstruct the chain by grouping its different operations. Activated RTK supplies docking sites, Grb2 supplies the adaptor link, and Sos supplies nucleotide exchange on Ras. Ras–GTP then **binds and activates Raf** through the interaction described in the course; Ras does not phosphorylate Raf as though it were a kinase.

The phosphorylation cascade begins with Raf acting on MEK and MEK acting on ERK. The hierarchy is Raf/MAPKKK, MEK/MAPKK, and ERK/MAPK. Active ERK phosphorylates downstream targets, including proteins and transcriptional regulators that alter the cellular response. This final activity is not extracellular ligand binding. Distinguishing docking, exchange, binding, and phosphate transfer gives each arrow its correct meaning and explains how the original receptor event is translated into a wider intracellular response.

## P5d-2

Repair both order and protein identity. The adaptor **Grb2 precedes Sos** in the receptor-to-Ras connection; Sos promotes Ras activation by nucleotide exchange. Ras is a **GTPase**, not a protein kinase. Its active form binds Raf, after which the kinase sequence is **Raf → MEK → ERK**, not the reversed ordering in the quoted statement.

ERK is an intracellular MAP kinase that phosphorylates downstream targets, including transcriptional regulators. It is not the extracellular ligand that started the sequence. The corrected chain therefore runs activated RTK, Grb2, Sos, Ras–GTP, Raf, MEK, ERK, then downstream responses. Writing the correct names without explaining their tasks would leave the Ras-kinase and ERK-ligand errors unresolved. Assigning one operation to each component repairs the mechanism as well as the memorized sequence.

## P5d-3

Follow the intact upstream events up to the block. Activated RTK recruits the Grb2–Sos arrangement; Sos promotes GDP release/GTP binding on Ras; and Ras–GTP can interact with Raf. However, Raf's kinase activity is needed to phosphorylate MEK. Blocking that activity prevents effective propagation through the next kinase step even while Ras remains active.

MEK activation therefore falls, followed by reduced ERK activation through this route. Ras cannot replace Raf by directly phosphorylating MEK or simply skipping to ERK, because its role is a GTP-dependent binding switch. Normally, active ERK phosphorylates downstream proteins and transcriptional regulators to generate the cellular response. The example shows why upstream ON and downstream inactivity can coexist when an intervening operation is blocked. It supports a directional prediction, not an exact residual-activity percentage.
