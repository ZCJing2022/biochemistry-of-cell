## w10-mc01

The location matters because electron transport must be connected to a gradient across a membrane. Respiratory proteins in the inner mitochondrial membrane move protons from the matrix side to the intermembrane-space side. ATP synthase in that same membrane can then use their return. The outer membrane does not define the proton barrier described here, and the animal plasma membrane and nuclear envelope are not the locations of this mitochondrial system. Remember the functional arrangement, not just the name: inner membrane separates the two compartments whose proton difference is used to make ATP.

## w10-mc08

## w10-mc09

## w10-mc16

## w10-mc17

ATP synthase spans a functional boundary between proton movement in the membrane and catalysis on the matrix side. Its F0 region is membrane embedded and participates in the proton-flow and rotary mechanism. The F1 head contains the catalytic sites discussed in the following question. Distinguishing those regions prevents assigning ADP-binding chemistry and membrane proton passage to the same structural description. F0 is not a soluble glycolytic enzyme, a DNA-binding protein, or oxygen's replacement as an electron acceptor. ## w10-mc18

The F1 region is the catalytic head in which the binding-change cycle produces and releases ATP from ADP and Pi. It responds to rotation of the internal shaft generated by proton movement through the membrane-associated part of the enzyme. Thus the catalytic region and proton-flow region perform complementary jobs. Mitochondrial DNA is not the catalytic component, GLUT2 is a glucose transporter from the liver discussion, and cytochrome c is an electron carrier rather than the ATP-forming head. ## w10-mc19

The rotation described is internal to ATP synthase. Proton flow drives rotor movement, including the central γ shaft. As that shaft changes its orientation relative to the catalytic head, the αβ units change conformation. The head should not be pictured as three catalytic units simply spinning together as a rigid wheel. Nor does the whole mitochondrion or Complex IV rotate to make ATP. The question tests how mechanical movement is transmitted to catalytic shape changes, so identifying both what rotates and what responds is essential.

## w10-mc20

Stationary does not mean inactive or unchanging. The αβ catalytic units remain in the head but change their conformations as the central shaft rotates. Those different conformations support binding ADP and Pi, favoring ATP formation, and releasing ATP. The option saying they do nothing overlooks the very changes responsible for catalysis. Hydrolyzing glucose and carrying oxygen are unrelated to their role in this machine. The important distinction is between large-scale rotation of the rotor and changes in shape within the catalytic units that respond to that rotation.

## w10-mc27

## w10-lo04

Trace energy conservation in two stages. First, electrons pass through the respiratory system toward oxygen. Some of the associated free-energy decrease is used by proton-pumping complexes to move H+ from matrix to intermembrane space. ## w10-lo05

Both contributions favor proton return toward the matrix under the conditions described. Naming only a pH gradient omits the electrical component, while naming only voltage omits the concentration component. ## w10-ix05

The two systems are linked by a shared intermediate energy store, not by identical chemistry. The ETC transfers electrons toward oxygen and uses part of the released energy to pump H+ across the inner membrane. ATP synthase does not take over that electron-transfer job. It uses the return of protons down the electrochemical gradient to drive its rotary catalytic mechanism and form ATP from ADP and Pi.
